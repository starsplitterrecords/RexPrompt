# REX FLEET — ISSUE 01
## A World Before the Story
### Before the Crossing

Distance used to be a fact of life. Then, for a long time, it became the fact that determined nearly everything else.

Humanity had spread among the stars along a network of engineered passages known as the corridors. A ship entering one of these carefully measured volumes could travel at speeds impossible in ordinary space. The passages were not shortcuts granted by a generous universe. They relied on reciprocal fields, precise timing, stable reference points, and people who knew the difference between a safe crossing and a reading that only appeared safe. Keeping them open was work. So was keeping the ships that used them alive.

Then came the Shattering.

The corridors did not simply grow inconvenient. Reliable fast travel broke down across the settled regions, and places that had depended on each other abruptly found themselves separated by journeys that could take months or years. Some routes remained possible at punishing cost. Others were lost, damaged, or too uncertain to trust. News traveled poorly. A shipment could arrive after the people who ordered it had moved, died, or learned to live without it. An emergency somewhere far away could become a historical footnote before anyone capable of helping heard about it.

Life went on because it had to.

At the center of the old network, the Core held on to much of its technical and military inheritance. Its Fleet protected what could still be reached, maintained vessels built for a larger civilization, and taught its officers a doctrine with two obligations: Reach and Resolve. Reach meant finding the way back to people. Resolve meant helping them endure when the way was gone. The officers who kept that promise through generations of isolation had reason to take pride in it. The people who waited for help that never arrived had reasons of their own.

Beyond the Core, the Verge developed a different understanding of survival. Its farms, markets, ports, local governments, and working escorts had to make decisions without expecting a central authority to solve them. A route belonged, in practice, to the people who knew how to fly it. A merchant's timetable could matter as much as a warship's orders. The Verge was not waiting to be civilized when the old connections returned. It had been governing, trading, repairing, and raising families all along.

Farther out, in places shaped by wreckage and scarcity, the Shards built communities from what the Shattering left behind. Their settlements have engineers and farmers, repair crews and children, captains and political authorities. They have also learned to take supplies from traffic they cannot always persuade to stop. Some call that piracy. Others can point to the empty holds and histories that made it seem necessary. Neither word can tell the entire story of the people living there.

Between all of them are independent traders, brokers, pilots, and the crews who keep routes moving when nobody agrees who ought to control them.

Now the corridors are beginning to reopen.

A passage that once consumed a generation's patience can suddenly become a practical journey again. For a family, it might mean a new home. For a farmer, a market that pays enough to keep next season's crop growing. For a settlement short of food, the sight of a freighter on the approach. For a navy, a chance to restore a service it believes civilization cannot survive without.

But reconnection does not return people to the moment when they were separated. The governments have changed. The borders have changed. People have learned what to fear, whom to trust, and what it costs to depend on someone else. Faster travel gives every old argument less distance in which to cool.

Even the corridors themselves remain demanding. No ship can bargain with shear, drift, or a failed timing reference. The mathematics can be checked. A hull can still strike debris. An arrival window can still close. And after the last course correction, someone must decide which vessel goes first and who will stand between it and danger.

On Luminara, a grain freighter is preparing to depart. Cargo crews are securing food grown by people who need the shipment to arrive. A mother and her son are taking their belongings aboard because somewhere beyond the crossing, they believe, there may be a better place to live.

The route is open again.

That is the beginning of the story, not the end of anyone's problems.
